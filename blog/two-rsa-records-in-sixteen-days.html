Two RSA Records Fell in Sixteen Days. Here Is What Changed.

We were preparing Factoring Quest: RSA Numbers for release, and we could hardly have picked a stranger moment.

For years, the upper end of the old RSA Factoring Challenge had been quiet. RSA-250, a 250-digit and 829-bit number, was factored in 2020. Then the public frontier stopped moving.

On September 3, 2026, RSA-260 fell. Sixteen days later, on September 19, RSA-896 followed. The public record had jumped from 829 bits to 862 bits, then from 862 to 896 bits. Two researchers, two AI systems and two enormous piles of GPU work were involved.

The obvious headline is that AI suddenly learned how to factor enormous numbers. That is not quite what happened. The mathematics barely changed. The engineering around it did.

First, what actually fell?

An RSA challenge number is simple to describe. Take two enormous prime numbers, p and q, and multiply them:

N = p × q

Going forward is easy. Given the two primes, a computer can multiply them almost instantly. Going backward is the hard part: given only N, recover p and q. That asymmetry is the mathematical idea behind RSA.

RSA Laboratories published a collection of specially generated numbers as public factoring challenges in 1991. RSA-260 contains 260 decimal digits and occupies 862 bits. On September 3, Cognition engineer Eric Lu published its two 130-digit prime factors, ending a challenge that had been open since the original list appeared.

Then came RSA-896. Despite the larger-looking name, it is only slightly bigger because it is named by its bit length, not its decimal length. It contains 270 decimal digits and is 896 bits long. On September 19, Stephen A. Weis published its two 135-digit factors. The result is independently checkable: multiply them and the original challenge number comes back.

No new factoring algorithm appeared

Neither result came from a newly discovered shortcut. Both used the General Number Field Sieve, or GNFS, the standard algorithm for the largest general-purpose integer factorizations. Both built on CADO-NFS, the mature open-source implementation used throughout the factoring community.

In his detailed account, Lu says there were essentially no algorithmic advances. The breakthrough was performance engineering: adapting and optimizing the existing pipeline for modern GPUs.

The expensive stages of GNFS contain enormous quantities of work that can be split into smaller jobs. Modern GPU clusters supply the parallel compute and memory bandwidth to make those jobs attractive. The obstacle was software capable of using that hardware efficiently.

RSA-260: old mathematics on new hardware

On August 13, Lu asked Cognition's Devin coding agent to build a GPU replacement for CADO-NFS's lattice-sieving component. Hours later, it had something working. Over the following weeks, multiple agent sessions optimized the sieve and much of the surrounding pipeline: polynomial selection, relation processing, linear algebra, square-root computation, scheduling and the infrastructure required to keep a distributed run alive.

The final computation consumed about 4,900 GPU-days, or 13.5 GPU-years of aggregate work. Lu estimated a market value of roughly $400,000 and reported a cost around ten times lower than the previous public state of the art.

GPU-years do not mean somebody waited thirteen years. Large parts of the job ran simultaneously. Thousands of pieces could be scattered across many GPUs, turning years of aggregate computation into weeks of wall-clock time.

Cognition also had fragmented compute: pieces of large clusters that were temporarily unused or awkward to assign to normal training workloads. Factoring is unusually good at consuming those leftovers. The combination of GPUs, spare capacity and an AI agent repeatedly rewriting and benchmarking the software was enough to move the record.

Sixteen days later

On September 19, Weis announced the factorization of RSA-896. Claude had helped port CADO-NFS to GPUs and orchestrate a fleet that grew as large as 2,048 GPUs. The work ran as a low-priority load in otherwise unused capacity.

It took about ten days of wall-clock time and represented approximately 30 GPU-years of aggregate computation. Again, this was not an AI looking at a 270-digit integer and guessing its factors. Claude acted more like a software engineer and systems operator: write code, compile it, benchmark it, find a bottleneck, rewrite it, launch jobs, recover from interruptions and repeat.

The number field sieve did the mathematics. The GPUs did the computation. The AI helped make the machinery work.

So why did this happen now?

Several things matured at once. The factoring algorithm was already known. CADO-NFS already provided an extraordinary body of expert software. Modern GPU clusters supplied immense parallel compute and memory bandwidth. AI companies happened to possess thousands of those GPUs, including fragments of capacity that were hard to use for their main workloads.

What remained expensive was the engineering effort required to make those pieces cooperate efficiently. AI agents compressed that loop. A specialized optimization that once demanded an unusual overlap of numerical software, GPU, distributed-systems and computational-number-theory expertise could now be explored by one person directing many agent sessions.

That does not eliminate human expertise. Lu still had to set goals, construct meaningful benchmarks, judge experiments, recognize bad directions and steer the work. But it changed how much implementation one person could explore in a short period.

There is one wonderfully confusing detail

Open Factoring Quest and you can still choose RSA-270. It remains unsolved. But did we not just say that a 270-digit RSA number had been factored? Yes. Welcome to RSA challenge naming.

Same decimal length. Different challenges.

RSA-270 Named for 270 decimal digits 895 bits · still open
RSA-896 Named for its 896-bit length 270 digits · factored

They are completely different numbers. The fact that the 896-bit number fell first does not give RSA-270 mysterious extra strength. Numbers this close in size inhabit roughly the same difficulty regime, while the result of a particular attempt depends on the target, parameters, implementation, available compute and many engineering details. It simply means nobody has published RSA-270's factors yet.

Does this mean RSA is broken?

No. Neither result is a factorization of a deployed RSA-2048 key. Weis explicitly says the RSA-896 work does not affect the security of deployed RSA-2048 keys. Lu likewise concludes that the reported efficiency gains do not make RSA-2048-sized GNFS factorizations practical.

The more interesting boundary is RSA-1024. Lu estimates that moving from RSA-260 to a 1024-bit modulus would require roughly 78 times as much computation and cost tens of millions of dollars at market GPU prices. Weis argues that 1024-bit RSA is within reach of actors with data-centre-scale GPU fleets.

That is not the same as saying RSA-1024 has now been publicly factored. It has not. Nor is 1024-bit RSA a modern recommended key size. September supplied something more specific: a visible demonstration of how much cheaper large classical factoring experiments can become when AI-assisted engineering meets hyperscale GPU infrastructure.

Why we built Factoring Quest

Factoring Quest: RSA Numbers does something deliberately much simpler. It does not contain GNFS. It does not rent thousands of GPUs. It does not pretend your iPhone is about to outperform a data centre.

Instead, it lets you touch the underlying problem. Choose one of the official RSA challenge moduli. Look at its size. See where its smaller prime factor must live. Generate candidates, sieve away obvious composites, divide the survivors into N and watch the remainder.

Most of the time, of course, the answer is not zero. It is almost unimaginably unlikely to be zero. That is the point.

The app performs genuine tests against the genuine challenge numbers while showing the odds exactly as they are. Its archive carries all 54 official moduli, separating the pieces of factoring history already solved from the ones still waiting.

The random search in the app is not how RSA-260 or RSA-896 were factored. Trial division asks one candidate at a time: does this number divide N? GNFS gathers billions of mathematical relationships until enough structure accumulates to expose a factor. One fits on a phone and makes the enormous search space visible. The other consumes GPU-years.

If exploring real cryptographic challenges is your kind of puzzle, try our flagship app, Bitcoin Puzzle Quest. It brings the public Bitcoin puzzle hunt to your iPhone, letting you test candidate private keys offline and explore the search ranges. The mathematical problem is different from RSA factoring, but the fascination is familiar: a known target, an enormous search space, and honest odds.

The challenge list is alive again

When RSA Laboratories ended the competition in 2007, the prizes disappeared. The numbers did not. They remained public, their decimal digits unchanged, waiting for algorithms, hardware and software to catch up.

For more than six years, the upper public record did not move. Then it moved twice in sixteen days. No quantum computer arrived. No secret formula made factoring easy. Old mathematics met new GPUs, mature open-source software met AI-assisted engineering, and unused fragments of enormous computing clusters became useful places to hunt for prime factors.

A difficult problem does not always become easier because somebody discovers a better idea. Sometimes the idea has been sitting there for thirty years. What changes is our ability to execute it.

And somewhere inside each remaining RSA challenge number, the missing pair of primes is already there. Waiting.
