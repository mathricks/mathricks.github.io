Why Random Search Leaves Gaps

There is a moment in Bitcoin Puzzle Quest that quietly breaks most people's intuition about probability.

You pick a puzzle. The app tells you how many candidate private keys its range contains. You start testing, and the counter climbs. Eventually the counter passes that number. You have now tested as many keys as the range holds.

So you are done, correct? Every key must have been tried at least once?

No. You have a 63.2 percent chance of having touched the one that matters, and a 36.8 percent chance of never having seen it at all.

This is not a quirk of one app. It is the central fact about searching by random sampling, and it has a clean derivation with a surprising amount of structure underneath it. Let us work through it.

The one line of algebra

Set up the problem precisely. There are N candidate keys in the range. Exactly one of them is the target. Each guess is drawn uniformly at random from all N candidates, independently of every guess before it. This is what statisticians call sampling with replacement: nothing stops you from drawing the same candidate twice, and nothing remembers that you already tried it.

A single draw misses the target with probability 1 − 1/N. Draws are independent, so the probability that all of n draws miss is that quantity raised to the n:

P(miss after n draws) = (1 − 1/N)n and therefore P(hit) = 1 − (1 − 1/N)n

That is the entire model. Everything else in this article is a consequence of that expression.

Now substitute the interesting case, the one that trips people up. Set n = N. You have spent exactly as many guesses as the range has candidates.

P(miss) = (1 − 1/N)N

Where 63.2 percent comes from

That expression is one of the classic definitions of e, the base of the natural logarithm. As N grows, (1 − 1/N)N converges to 1/e.

1/e = 0.367879441…
1 − 1/e = 0.632120558…

So spending a full key-space worth of random guesses buys you a 63.2 percent chance of success, not 100 percent. The missing 36.8 percent is the probability mass that went into repeats. Every time a draw lands on a candidate you already tested, that guess bought you nothing. Those wasted draws have to come out of somewhere, and where they come out of is your coverage.

The number is not an artifact of large key-spaces or floating-point rounding. It is what happens when you search without memory. A memoryless search cannot spend all of its effort on new ground, because it has no way to know which ground is new.

It is a floor, not an approximation

Here is a detail that matters more than it first appears, and that most write-ups skip.

1 − 1/e is the limit as N goes to infinity. But the approach to that limit is monotone. The miss probability (1 − 1/N)N increases with N, which means the hit probability decreases with N, converging on 63.212 percent from above and never dipping below it.

Work the smallest case by hand to see why. Take N = 2: two candidates, one of which is the target, and you are allowed two draws. A single draw misses with probability 1/2. Two draws both miss with probability 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, because the draws are independent. So the chance of having hit the target is 1 − 1/4 = 3/4, or 75 percent. The 25 percent you lose is precisely the chance you drew the same wrong candidate twice.

Every row below is that same calculation with different numbers. Substitute N into (1 − 1/N)N to get the miss probability, then subtract from 1.

Key-space N Miss = (1 − 1/N)N P(hit) = 1 − miss
2 (1/2)2 = 0.250000 75.0000%
4 (3/4)4 = 0.316406 68.3594%
16 (15/16)16 = 0.356074 64.3926%
1,024 (1023/1024)1024 = 0.367700 63.2300%
1,048,576 (1 − 1/220)220 = 0.367879 63.2121%
N → ∞ 1/e = 0.367879… 63.2121%

Reading down the middle column shows the convergence directly: 0.25, 0.316, 0.356, 0.3677, 0.36788, each one closer to 1/e than the last and none of them past it.

This is why the correct thing to tell a user is "at least 63 percent" rather than "about 63 percent". For any finite key-space, from a two-candidate toy range up to the 2134 candidates of Puzzle 135, the statement holds exactly. It is a proven bound, not a rounded estimate, and bounds are much better things to put in a user interface than estimates.

The whole curve

Nothing forces you to stop at one key-space worth of guesses. Write n = cN, where c is how many times over you have covered the range, and the expression collapses into something clean:

P(hit) = 1 − (1 − 1/N)cN ≈ 1 − e−c the approximation is excellent for any N above a few thousand

There is a second, equivalent way to reach the same formula, and it is worth knowing because it generalises better. The number of times your n draws land on the target follows a binomial distribution with n trials and success probability 1/N. When n is large and 1/N is small, that binomial converges to a Poisson distribution with mean λ = n/N = c. The Poisson probability of zero events is e−λ, which is exactly the miss probability above. Random key search is a Poisson process, and the 63.2 percent figure is just P(at least one event) when the expected count is 1.

That is the curve at the top of this article, and its shape is the whole story: the steep part is over almost immediately, and the flat part goes on forever. Here it is as numbers.

Keys tested P(hit) = 1 − e−c Still missed
0.5 × N 39.35% 60.65%
1 × N 63.21% 36.79%
2 × N 86.47% 13.53%
3 × N 95.02% 4.98%
5 × N 99.33% 0.67%
10 × N 99.995% 0.005%

The decay is exactly exponential, and that is the problem

Look at the "still missed" column. Each additional key-space worth of work multiplies it by the same constant:

1/e ≈ 0.3679 every extra N draws removes 63.2 percent of the remaining doubt, never more

This is the same structure as radioactive decay or a half-life, and it has the same consequence. The quantity approaches zero, but it has no last step. There is no number of random draws, however large, that makes the miss probability zero. A random search over a finite space is an infinite process.

That is a strange thing to say about a search of a finite set. Search the same set methodically and you are guaranteed to finish. Search it randomly and you are guaranteed never to finish. The set did not change. Only the memory did.

Coverage and hit probability are the same number

A pleasant identity falls out here. Ask a different question: after n random draws, how many distinct candidates have you actually seen?

Each individual candidate escapes all n draws with probability (1 − 1/N)n. By linearity of expectation, summing over all N candidates:

E[distinct keys seen] = N ⋅ (1 − e−c)

Which is the same factor as before. So "you have a 63.2 percent chance of having hit the target" and "you have seen 63.2 percent of the range" are not two facts that happen to share a number. They are one fact stated twice. The target is not special. It is an arbitrary member of the set, and your chance of having found it is precisely the fraction of the set you managed to cover.

This is why coverage is the honest progress metric for a random search, and why any display built on "keys tested" alone will eventually mislead. Keys tested is an input. Coverage is the output, and after the first key-space they diverge badly.

Your first repeat arrives absurdly early

People assume repeats are a late-stage problem, something that only starts to bite once you have covered a meaningful fraction of the range. The opposite is true, and the relevant result is the birthday problem.

The expected number of draws before you see your first collision is not proportional to N. It is proportional to the square root of N:

E[draws to first repeat] ≈ √(πN / 2)

Puzzle 71 has an interval of 270 candidates, roughly 1.18 sextillion. The square root term puts your first duplicate key at around 43 billion draws. That is about one part in 27 billion of the range: for all practical purposes, the very beginning of the search. You will be drawing keys you have already drawn long before you have covered any noticeable slice of the space. (Wikipedia)

Individually, each repeat is harmless. Collectively, they are the entire reason the curve flattens.

What actually finishing would cost

If repeats are the obstacle, the natural question is how many random draws it would take to see every candidate at least once. This is the coupon collector's problem, and its answer is famously worse than people expect:

E[draws to see all N] = N ⋅ (ln N + γ) + ½ γ ≈ 0.5772, the Euler–Mascheroni constant

The ln N factor is the sting. For Puzzle 71, ln(270) is about 48.5, so full coverage by random sampling would take roughly 49 key-spaces of work, about 5.8 × 1022 draws. (Wikipedia)

And because the factor is logarithmic in N, it grows with the puzzle number. At Puzzle 135 the multiplier is not 49 but about 93. Random search does not just fail to finish. The margin by which it fails gets steadily worse as the problem gets harder.

The factor of two nobody mentions

Put the two search strategies side by side, and ask how long each takes to find the key on average.

For random sampling with replacement, the number of draws until the first hit is geometrically distributed with success probability 1/N, so the expected number of draws is N.

For a sequential sweep with no repeats, the target sits at a uniformly random position in the sweep, so the expected number of draws is (N + 1) / 2.

A methodical sweep finds the key in half the work, on average, and comes with a guaranteed finish. Random sampling costs twice as much and guarantees nothing.

A sweep asks for two things a hand-tapped random guess does not: a starting point you choose deliberately, and a run that keeps going without you. In Bitcoin Puzzle Quest those arrive together in Continuous Mode, which takes a start key and scans deterministically upward from it while the app does the tapping. Worth being clear about the ceiling, though: halving the work on a range of 270 still leaves 269, so no strategy makes the high puzzles tractable on a phone. The factor of two is worth having at the scale where finishing is genuinely on the table.

Stated that baldly, random sampling still looks indefensible for everyone else. So why does it remain the default in Bitcoin Puzzle Quest, and in serious distributed key-search efforts generally?

Why random anyway

Because the assumption behind the comparison is wrong. There is not one searcher.

A sequential sweep is only efficient if every participant sweeps a different stretch. The instant two searchers start from the same place, all of the second one's work is duplicated, and its efficiency advantage becomes an efficiency disaster. Keeping sweeps disjoint requires assigning ranges, tracking which ranges are claimed, detecting abandoned ranges and reissuing them. In other words, it requires a coordinator, shared state and trust that participants report honestly.

Random sampling needs none of that. It is stateless. Two players who never communicate, on opposite sides of the planet, overlap only by chance, and their combined coverage still follows the same curve with n as the pooled total. You can add a searcher, or ten thousand, with no registration and no bookkeeping.

The factor of two is the price of needing no coordinator. For a permissionless, offline, uncoordinated search, that is usually a bargain, which is why random draws are what you get by default and why they are the right tool when you are hunting alone and unannounced.

The moment you stop being alone, the arithmetic flips. Two friends who agree that one starts at the bottom of a range and the other at the midpoint have solved the overlap problem with a single sentence, no server required, and between them they cover the range without a single duplicated draw. Two random searchers, pooling the same effort, are back on the 1 − e−c curve and its repeats. That is the case a deterministic sweep is built for: pick your stretch, say so, and let it run.

Two different questions, two different numbers

Almost every misunderstanding in this area comes from mixing up two quantities that both get called "the odds".

  • The odds on the next draw. This is 1/N. It was 1/N on your first guess and it is still 1/N on your trillionth. It never improves. Not by one part in a billion.
  • The odds accumulated so far. This is 1 − e−c. It rises with every guess, quickly at first and then barely at all.

The geometric distribution is memoryless, which is the formal statement of the fact that the first number never moves. A long streak of failures does not mean you are "due". The key-space does not know how long you have been trying, and the sampler does not either. What your effort buys is entirely captured by the second number, and the second number is the one worth displaying.

The takeaway

Randomness is a remarkable tool for search. It parallelises without coordination, needs no memory, cannot be gamed by an adversary who knows your strategy, and requires no infrastructure whatsoever. Those properties are why it shows up everywhere from Monte Carlo integration to load balancing to cryptographic key hunts.

But it buys those properties with a specific, quantifiable inefficiency, and 1/e is the exchange rate. Spend a key-space, keep 36.8 percent of your doubt. Spend another, keep 13.5 percent. Spend forever, keep something.

A methodical search ends. A random search only fades.

If you want to watch the curve behave in the wild, Bitcoin Puzzle Quest is an offline simulator for exactly this process. Tap a key and you are sampling; the odds above are the odds you are playing. If the arithmetic has left you preferring the other half of the trade, sweeping over sampling, that is what Continuous Mode does: you name the starting key and it walks the range from there, no repeats and no tapping.
